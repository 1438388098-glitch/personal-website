## Research question

English common law has long been narrated as the "exception" outside the tide of the Roman law revival: after the Norman Conquest, England's legal order grew independently out of custom and precedent, having nothing to do with the Continental tradition of Roman law. This Foreign Legal History course paper tests that narrative: to what extent did Bracton's 13th-century *On the Laws and Customs of England* inherit its conceptual framework and systematic architecture from the Bolognese glossators (above all from Azo's works)? Is "common law exceptionalism" an objective description of history, or a narrative constructed by later generations?

## Core argument

Bracton's borrowing from Roman law was a "framework borrowing": the prologue transplants concepts such as *lex*, *consuetudo*, *iustitia* and *aequitas*, along with the tripartite scheme of persons, things and actions; but the absolute ownership (*dominium*) of Roman law will not fit into England's feudal tenure — land belongs in theory to the king, vassals hold only "tenure" conditioned on service, and *seisin*, the Anglo-Saxon protection of possession, does not map onto the Roman *possessio*. The text therefore preserves three mutually contradictory schemes of integration, and they record a head-on collision between two legal logics. In common law's borrowing of Roman legal science — the move from case experience toward a theoretical system — this book is the landmark step.

## Line of argument

Six chapters, unfolding along "narrative - sources - evidence - limits - reflection":

1. **The crack in the exceptionalist narrative.** Bracton (Henry de Bracton, c. 1210–1268) was a royal judge under Henry III, sitting as an itinerant justice from 1245; from the plea rolls of the royal courts he selected more than 2,000 cases to compile *Bracton's Note Book*, and between 1250 and 1260 he wrote *On the Laws and Customs of England* in Latin. The earliest work to systematize English law appeared exactly where Roman law's influence was most concentrated — the standard narrative cracks open here.
2. **The Roman law revival and the glossators.** Irnerius (c. 1055–1130) founded the school of glossators at Bologna, and from then on legal science broke away from rhetoric as an independent discipline; Azo (c. 1150–1230) wrote the *Summa on the Code* and the *Summa on the Institutes*, and the saying of the time went that without reading Azo's books one could not enter the palace. In 1205 the Englishman Thomas of Marlborough traveled to Bologna and heard Azo's lectures for six months, calling him "the master of masters of all legal science" — the channel existed.
3. **Maitland's discovery.** In 1862 the German scholar Guterbock first argued that much of Bracton's material came from Azo; in 1895 Maitland edited *Bracton and Azo* for the Selden Society, setting the two texts side by side, and settled the judgment. Maine, in *Ancient Law*, charged that Bracton had "borrowed the whole form and a third of the contents of his work directly from the Corpus Juris Civilis"; Maitland called this "an astonishing exaggeration" and gave the calibration: what was borrowed directly from the Corpus Juris Civilis is less than a thirtieth of the whole book, and what came indirectly through Azo about a fifteenth; Book I (about 106 of the 444 folio pages) corresponds to Justinian's *Institutes* — even the definition of justice as "the constant and perpetual wish to render everyone his due" is transcribed straight from Azo's gloss.
4. **The limits of Roman law.** Following the multi-author relay reconstruction established via McSweeney: Martin of Pattishall began the text, William of Raley wrote most of it, and Bracton supplemented it in the 1250s as Raley's clerk, with the main body essentially complete before 1236. The text's stratification explains why the three schemes of integration contradict one another.
5. **The construction of the exceptionalist story.** The book's first printing in 1569 stirred controversy at once: Fitzherbert stated plainly that what Bracton wrote hardly counted as "our law", and Fulbecke warned that the book was "dangerous" and that "the greatest part of that law is now obsolete"; in the early 17th century Coke cited it selectively to prove that English law had been so since ancient times, and only after the 1640 reprint was its canonical status established. The "meaning" of a medieval manuscript is made by later legal communities interpreting it continuously.
6. **Conclusion.** English law's distinctiveness took shape in interaction with the European legal community, and Roman law supplied its scholarly language; tension and reworking together form the most decisive intellectual drive in the making of the common law.

## A further thought

Legal transplantation happens at the level of conceptual frameworks and scholarly method, and then, digested and reworked by local institutions, grows its own shape — a judgment that reaches beyond medieval history; it is also the premise from which I approach the comparative-law questions of today.
