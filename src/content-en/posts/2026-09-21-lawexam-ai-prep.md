On September 12 and 13, the objective phase of the 2026 National Unified Legal Professional Qualification Examination opened, covering 31 provincial-level regions, the Xinjiang Production and Construction Corps, and the Hong Kong and Macau exam zones. Jilin reported nearly 18,000 registered candidates, Chongqing more than 16,000, Hainan over 8,000. A detail from the Chongqing exam-site coverage: candidates queued through security while proctors with handheld metal detectors checked them one by one, focusing on "smart glasses and similar devices".

Smart glasses getting named by officials may be the year's most symbolic piece of exam news. While the halls guard against AI coming in, prep outside has long been a different picture: wrong-answer organizing is handed to AI summarization, drilling and scoring to programs, and even the memorization schedule is laid out by an algorithm. One exam, two sets of rules, inside and out.

## The changes outside the hall hide in the registration data

The Inner Mongolia Department of Justice reported a set of figures: this year the region's candidates run from 71 years old at the oldest to 19 at the youngest, and people from outside the legal industry make up 62.5% of registrants. The bar exam's candidate pool has expanded from legal professionals to every trade across society.

These figures matter for understanding AI-driven prep. A working person switching into law has no classmate group, no library study room, no study partners to quiz each other; the entire prep environment is the handful of tools on their phone. For such candidates, AI prep tools are not icing on the cake — they are the only sparring partner. Tool quality directly determines prep quality, and this "business" weighs more than it looks.

## What the hall blocks and what candidates use are two different AIs

Put what the hall strictly guards against next to what is popular outside it, and you find they are not the same kind of thing at all.

The hall guards against "answering for you": a device comes in, searches for you, outputs for you. It attacks the exam's reliability, which is why radio monitoring and metal detectors are worth deploying against it.

What candidates use daily is "preparing with you": sorting the questions you got wrong, tallying why you got them wrong, compressing hundreds of pages of lecture notes into memorizable cards. What it replaces is mechanical labor; forming judgment still happens inside your own head.

I built two bar-exam tools with my own hands this year, and I feel this dividing line keenly. One is a wrong-answer-notebook makeover: washing Zhuma's wrong-answer data into memorizable notes — all the AI does is hauling and recombining; the memorizing, I do myself. The other is a scoring tool: an Agent grades my subjective-question practice. Designing it, the failure mode I feared most was the "fake green light": AI judges that you have mastered something, you believe it, and only in the exam hall do you discover you had not. So its output is always an evidence chain: which sentence fell short, which exam point it maps to; the scoring rationale must be laid out for human review, and it never hands down a bare "pass".

## The essence of the line: tools cannot form memory for you

Following the line, the value boundary of AI prep tools is actually clear: it can outsource the repetitive labor of prep to the limit, but forming memory and understanding has no outsourcing option. What the exam tests is precisely "what remains in your head once every aid is gone".

It is also why I stay skeptical of "AI question-prediction" products. Question prediction aims at luck; scoring aims at feedback quality; summarizing aims at time. The former's returns go to zero as exam writers harden against prediction; the latter two accumulate your own ability — completely different ledgers.

## Closing

Objective-phase scores were published on September 19, and the subjective phase opens on October 18. The subjective phase tests argumentation — the hardest part to cheat with a device, and the one that feeds most on everyday grinding.

One piece of plain advice to my fellow candidates this year: use every bit of AI worth using; outsource the whole lot — hunting web pages for explanations, hand-copying wrong answers. But the "why" of each question has to stay in your own head and walk into the hall with you. The hall guards against those glasses; it cannot guard what your head contains. That is where this exam is truly fair.
