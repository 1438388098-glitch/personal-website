Near the end of December 2024, the Tongzhou District People's Court in Beijing heard a dispute over nominee shareholding. After the hearing, counsel for the plaintiff mailed in supplementary written arguments with two "reference cases" attached: one presented as coming from the Supreme People's Court, the other carrying the Shanghai First Intermediate Court docket number (2022) Hu 01 Min Zhong No. 12345. In both, the facts, the issues and the reasoning fit the case at trial so snugly that every one of the plaintiff's claims seemed backed by precedent. Judge Zheng Jizhe recalled that his clerk, at first review, was stunned and rated the references highly. Then they looked the docket number up. It is real; the case behind it is a private-lending dispute, nothing to do with nominee shareholding. Questioned, the lawyer admitted the workflow: distill the case's facts into prompts, feed them repeatedly to an LLM, copy the generated "cases", and mail them to the court.

More followed, some of it blunter. In a lease dispute in Dawu County, Hubei, the plaintiff's side submitted, as promised, photos of utility meters, and the photos carried a "generated by Doubao AI" watermark in the corner; pressed on details, the story fell apart. The court admonished the submitter and excluded the evidence. In an inheritance case before Beijing's Second Intermediate Court in September 2025, a party sent in "authorities found by AI"; judge Tu Yu checked them one by one and not a single one was an accurate statement of current law. Judge Shen Yongyan of Shanghai's Second Intermediate Court described a labor dispute closed in November 2025 where the provisions cited in the appeal, once checked, either do not exist or do not mean what the appeal says. In another appeal that reached the same court, judge Chen Ying found a citation to a "Judicial Interpretation II on several issues concerning the trial of social insurance disputes" purportedly issued in March 2025; after checking with senior colleagues, the interpretation turned out to be entirely invented.

## Why courts make good targets

What generative models do best is exactly what litigation paperwork demands: correct format, confident tone, content fitted to the case you supply. What most alarmed the court in the Tongzhou case was how perfectly the two fake cases "restated" the facts and claims of the live one. Real precedents are never this accommodating.

Reference cases genuinely carry weight. The rules for running the SPC's case database require courts at every level to search it and consider its cases when trying similar disputes, and for an uncommon claim a judge hopes for precedent too. Precisely because catalogued cases pull real weight, forging them pays.

The cost of verification sits on the human side. A judge has neither the duty nor the time to run a full-database check on every set of written arguments. The Tongzhou forgery was caught by professional instinct; Zheng's description was that the format "looked like early AI output, solid bullets on the left, and the parties were vague". Tu Yu found the wipeout only by checking provision by provision. Until someone stops to check, a well-formatted, fully cited fake is indistinguishable from the real thing.

The fraud takes no technique at all. The middle step is copy-paste.

## Courts everywhere are paying the same tuition

In 2023, two lawyers before the Southern District of New York submitted six cases invented by ChatGPT; the judge fined each of them 5000 dollars. In June 2025, the High Court of England and Wales handled two cases in a row where lawyers had cited AI-invented authorities; the judge referred the two lawyers to their regulators and warned the profession in writing. At the end of the same year, in proceedings involving Qatar National Bank, the judge counted 45 citations in a filing and found 18 to be unsubstantiated.

The models have been replaced several times over; the hallucination has not.

## Courts are turning this into rules

The response is layered. Case by case, the Tongzhou court refused to adopt that part of the arguments and named the conduct in the judgment: do not let AI models generate or fabricate false information and disturb judicial order. Dawu admonished the party and excluded the forged evidence. As rules, the Tongzhou case entered the SPC's case database under the entry "handling of false reference cases submitted through AI by participants in litigation without verification", docket 2025-18-2-494-001, there for any court to follow. As mechanism, Beijing's Second Intermediate Court runs its "three-all" reasoning review, putting materials and legal arguments submitted by parties under strict examination.

Zheng summed up three tells: docket numbers with a formulaic pattern; facts tailored too perfectly to the need; cases that cannot be verified by search. None of this is deep. A database search does it. What is hard is getting everyone who files papers to do it before pressing send.

## Make verification the default

Verification is, fittingly, an engineering problem. In the citation guard I built for my own legal-QA tooling, [legal-hallu-guard](/projects/legal-hallu-guard/), hallucinated citations are split into three classes and intercepted separately: a cited provision number that does not exist in the statute corpus; a quotation that does not match the provision's text; a key assertion with no source. All three checks are deterministic rules; no model sits in the loop. Across 550 adversarial test cases the interception rate is 100% for all three classes, with zero false kills on legitimate provisions.

The judge's three tells and my three rule classes are two faces of the same list: formulaic dockets match non-existent provisions; over-fitting matches unsourced assertions; hard-to-verify is what the guard does all day. The difference is who runs it and when: after filing, on judicial instinct, the cost lands on the courts; before filing, inside the tool, the cost is close to zero.

One copy-paste is all it takes for a hallucination to reach the courtroom. The defense has to be cheap enough to be on by default.
