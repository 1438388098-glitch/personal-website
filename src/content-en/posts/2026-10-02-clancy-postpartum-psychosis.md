On the evening of January 24, 2023, in Duxbury, Massachusetts, Patrick Clancy went out to pick up takeout and the children's medicine. When he came home, the house was quiet. The three children were in the basement with exercise resistance bands around their necks: Cora was five, Dawson three, Callan eight months. Cora and Dawson died that day; Callan died three days later. Their mother Lindsay fell from a second-floor window, injured her thoracic spine, and has been paralyzed below the waist since.

More than three years on, the case still has no verdict. It opened at Plymouth Superior Court on July 20, 2026 and was tried for over a month; at the end of August it went to the jury, and on September 4 the judge declared a mistrial, the jury deadlocked — reportedly 11 to 1, with the majority inclined to find her not criminally responsible. On October 1, the judge denied the defense's request for a required finding of not guilty on grounds of insufficient evidence; on November 2 the court will sit again to take up the motion to dismiss the charges on double-jeopardy grounds, the dissenting juror, and the question of a retrial.

My judgment: the evidence is sufficient to support first-degree murder convictions, and that 11-to-1 deadlock was the result of sympathy pulling it off course. The reasons follow.

## Only mental state remains in dispute

In April 2026, the defense proposed a written stipulation acknowledging that she killed the children, seeking to trade for an arrangement splitting the trial into two phases, "act" and "mental state"; the judge did not agree. So the factual side of this case has long been free of suspense, and the entire dispute presses on her mental state at the moment of the acts.

This is a question evidence can answer; it need not rest on inner conviction alone. The evidence comes from her own treatment records.

## The treatment records contain no psychotic symptoms

Lay out the weeks between her last clinical visits and the deaths:

- **January 1**: admitted to McLean Hospital; discharged five days later;
- **January 23**, the day before the deaths: one 17-minute psychiatric outpatient visit, where clinician Jennifer Tufts saw no psychotic symptoms and no mania;
- **During treatment**: Rebecca Jollotta, a nurse practitioner at South Shore Hospital's perinatal behavioral health clinic, testified that she never suspected postpartum psychosis;
- **January 26**: still intubated, she communicated in writing with forensic psychiatrist Jhilam Biswas, who observed no psychotic symptoms or hallucinations;
- **January 30**: Sejal Shah, a physician at Brigham and Women's Hospital, recorded that she denied suicidal and homicidal intent, denied hallucinations, and that her thinking was "organized and goal-directed"; on cross-examination, Shah agreed she was an honest patient.

All of these are records from treaters and neutral parties. Now consider the defense's experts: forensic psychologist Paul Zeizel conceded on cross-examination that before the deaths she did describe intrusive thoughts, but she never called them "voices telling her to kill"; his information about "the voice" came from others' retellings, including a priest's.

In other words, not one item of the case's most crucial exculpatory fact — the hallucination "commanding her to kill the children" — comes from her own clinical record.

## Medication worsening her condition does not equal exemption from responsibility

The defense's strongest card is the medication. From October 2022 to January 2023, doctors at multiple institutions prescribed her 13 psychiatric medications; the prescriptions were scattered and never in conversation with one another, and one hospital even documented "adverse drug reactions" in her chart and recommended dose reductions. In January 2026, she and her husband sued these medical providers separately.

These claims deserve a serious hearing, but that is the civil suit's business, trying whether the medical providers were at fault; the criminal case asks one question only: at the moment of the acts, did she still have the capacity to control her own conduct. The two questions cannot substitute for each other.

Prosecution expert Kirk Heilbrun's position is exactly this. He concedes that sleep deprivation and medication reactions worsened her condition, while holding that she retained the capacity for control — she could decide when, where and by what means to act — and he classifies her state as "altruistic filicide", falling short of acute psychosis. The toxicology points the same direction: the drug at the highest concentration in the autopsies was quetiapine, roughly tenfold below the toxic threshold, and forensic toxicologist Justin Brower testified that this does not fit the profile of a suicidal overdose.

That she was switched between medications repeatedly, slept badly for a long stretch, and lost hold of her emotions — that is true. But the same timeline carries another set of facts: on the afternoon of the day of the deaths, she took Cora to the pediatrician and played with the children in the snow; and before her husband left the house, it was she who sent him out for the takeout and the children's medicine.

## The conduct itself carried goals

The behavioral evidence the prosecution presented includes: in August 2022 she visited a website describing suicide methods and searched "how to cut a throat fatally"; before the deaths she searched words related to hallucinations; she calculated her husband's round-trip time for his errand. The three children died of strangulation, and the medical examiner testified that death by strangulation takes minutes of sustained pressure.

This was not a momentary loss of control. For all three children to die of strangulation, each one required starting over, sustaining the force, and then moving to the next.

## Three jurisdictions all keep the opening narrow

The Infanticide Act 《杀婴罪法》 of 1938 in England and Wales is a rare provision written specifically for the "postpartum" situation. It provides: where a mother's mind is disturbed by reason of her not having fully recovered from childbirth, or by reason of the effects of lactation consequent on childbirth, and she causes the death of her infant under one year of age, this is infanticide, punished as manslaughter. Two things deserve note. First, it covers only infants under one year — of this case's three children, only eight-month-old Callan falls inside its range. Second, even in England and Wales the prosecution would proceed, merely on a lesser offense.

Massachusetts runs a general test, established in 1967 by the state's highest court in McHoul (352 Mass. 544), descended from Section 4.01(1) of the 《模范刑法典》 (Model Penal Code): the actor must "lack substantial capacity" to appreciate the wrongfulness of her conduct or to conform her conduct to the requirements of law. China runs 《刑法》 (the Criminal Law) Article 18, requiring that at the time of the act the person "is unable to recognize or unable to control her own conduct".

The three jurisdictions word it differently but mean the same: a postpartum psychosis diagnosis alone does not discharge responsibility; for exemption, one must first prove the fact of "capacity lost". In this case, not one of the doctors who treated her said she had lost the capacity to recognize or to control.

## The support is selective

After the deaths, rallies of hundreds in pink shirts appeared outside Plymouth Superior Court. The TV pundits took sides too: per Boston.com, among conservatives some said she committed no crime (Candace Owens) and some advocated executing her (Matt Walsh); Ted Cruz called her a "monster mother", and the defense lawyer publicly fired back at the phrase.

This support crosses party lines and cannot simply be filed under left or right. It is closer to a selective sympathy: many mothers struggle inside postpartum psychosis, and the poor ones, the non-white ones, the ones who cannot afford a lawyer like Kevin Reddington, usually get prison, and a name nobody forwards. Turning a specific person into a movement costs the remaining ones their continued invisibility.

## Back to November 2

That hearing takes up procedural questions: the dissenting juror, the dismissal motion on double-jeopardy grounds, whether to retry. But the question to be answered already has its materials in the case file: whether a woman switched between medications repeatedly through the postpartum months and severely deprived of sleep lost, at that moment, the capacity to recognize or control her own conduct.

My judgment is that she did not lose it to the degree the law requires. From the same body of evidence, eleven of twelve people reached the opposite conclusion — a measure of how much weight sympathy carried in that jury room. That point is more worth recording than the verdict itself.
