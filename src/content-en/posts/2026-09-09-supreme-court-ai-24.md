In March, the Nanjing Intermediate People's Court upheld the first-instance judgment on appeal: Baidu's AI chat answer fabricated that a lawyer had "been sentenced to three years for the crime of explosion", constituting defamation, and Baidu would apologize in writing.

Six months later, on September 7, the Supreme People's Court issued the Opinion on Adjudicating AI-Related Disputes According to Law, Fa Fa [2026] No. 10, five parts and 24 articles. It is the first time China's highest adjudicating authority has issued unified adjudication rules for AI-related disputes, and the official reading pairs "AI hallucination" torts with "AI face-swapping and voice-cloning" as focus problems.

Put the two events side by side and a complete path of Chinese judicial response to new technology comes into view: individual cases walk first, rules follow.

## What the 24 articles carry

The structure first. Part one is guiding thought plus three basic principles: people first, supporting innovation, holding the security line. Part two (articles 3 to 11) governs torts: after fixing the attribution principles, it legislates by scenario (personality rights, personal information, consumer interests), with generative-AI provider liability, AI product liability and autonomous-driving accident compensation all here. Part three (articles 12 to 16) governs intellectual property across five areas: infringement determination, open-source software liability, patent grant and confirmation, technology contracts, data-use norms. Part four governs procedure: fact-finding, evidence review, and rules against misusing AI to collect evidence or submit false materials. Part five is the adjudication work mechanism.

The scenarios the Opinion names each map to a recent headline: AI face-swapping and voice cloning, AI hallucination, doxxing, big-data price discrimination, celebrity-impersonation livestreams, autonomous driving. Judicial documents are rarely this "news-shaped", which is itself evidence that these disputes have piled up on court desks for real.

## Two blanks in the Civil Code, now filled

For law students, the highest-value refinements are in the personality-rights section.

One is voice. Article 1023 of the Civil Code said only that "protection of a natural person's voice applies mutatis mutandis the provisions on portrait rights". The Opinion turns that into concrete elements: using a natural person's voice as training material without consent, imitating timbre, intonation and pronunciation style, and generating an identifiable synthetic voice, infringes voice interests. The dubbing actor's two-year-old question, "was my voice stolen?", now has an answer.

The other is the deceased. Against the "AI resurrection" phenomenon, the Opinion provides that processing a specific natural person's or a deceased person's name, portrait and the like with generative AI must not violate laws or public order and good morals. The backdrop is not abstract: in April this year, Dilraba Dilmurat's studio disclosed that related cases had reached 40, with 9 effective judgments from the Beijing Internet Court, AI face-swapping infringement among them. Ordinary people's digital likenesses forged at scale, the deceased "resurrected" to sell goods without family consent: the courts pulled all of it into the reach of personality interests.

## Fault liability still, but "fault" is getting concrete

The Opinion does nothing radical on liability attribution: the fault-liability principle stands. No surprise: the AI industry is early, and strict liability would dump the whole industry's risk onto platforms; the legislature never intended that.

But read March's Baidu judgment and you see the element of "fault" being re-concretized. In that case the judge questioned in open court: asked the same question, why did Doubao and DeepSeek produce no such statements? That question turns "fault" from an abstraction into an operational controlled experiment: content no other model generates, yours does, and you have nothing to say about your investment in hallucination governance.

For developers this is a solid signal: engineering investment in hallucination governance is becoming litigation evidence. Retrieval augmentation, citation verification, output evaluation, the things I build into my own tools (my statute validator detects fabricated citations at 100% over a 14,212-row corpus) are in court the evidence chain of "reasonable care taken". Engineering and law use different words for it; the checklists are strikingly alike.

## Open-source software gets its own article

One easily missed item: the IP part lists open-source software liability separately. The fair scope of training data, the judicial attitude to open-source licenses, questions developer communities have argued for years, formally entered the highest adjudication rules. The Opinion also sets the reasonable boundary for using publicly available personal information in model training. For the bamboo-shoots wave of Chinese open-source models, the rules arrived right on time.

## Closing

From one apology letter in March to 24 articles in September, six months. The individual case set a price (what that letter was worth); the rules set a boundary (under what conditions the next letter comes). Formal AI legislation is still on the road, and the Supreme People's Court unified adjudication standards through a judicial document first: the Chinese-style "judiciary moves first".

For a law student this is a living specimen of law responding to technology: you do not need to wait for a code to be born to watch the law move.
