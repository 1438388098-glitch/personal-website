On April 2, the National Medical Products Administration issued 《关于「人工智能+药品监管」的实施意见》 — the implementation opinions on "artificial intelligence plus drug regulation". A little over three months later, on July 23, the CSRC stated at its supervision work symposium that it will advance the application of AI in regulation.

Put the two news items together and the signal is clear: after a year or two of discussing "how to regulate AI", the regulators have started equipping themselves with AI. That is exactly why this NMPA document is worth reading: the constraints it writes for itself deserve more scrutiny than the ones it writes for the industry.

## An instruction manual written for itself

First, the NMPA's task list for itself: in review and approval, dedicated large models and agents do intelligent screening of application dossiers and automatic report generation; in production, for high-risk categories such as vaccines and blood products, agents dynamically monitor production video and IoT sensor data; in distribution, upgrade whole-catalog, whole-process traceability; on the government-services side, intelligent Q&A and smart pre-filling; on the enforcement side, use big data to grade risk levels and run precision inspections, rolling out mobile enforcement built on "scan the code when entering a firm".

The timetable is set too: by 2030, initially build an innovation system fusing regulation with AI; by 2035, basically form a smart governance pattern driven by data and intelligence.

On its own, this is a standard digitalization plan. The interesting parts are two qualifiers that recur through the document.

## "Human review, full audit trail" cuts both ways

The first qualifier: wherever AI assists review and approval, it must come with a human-machine coordination mechanism of "human review, full audit trail".

Anyone familiar with regulatory documents will notice that this phrasing is usually what regulators demand of the regulated: firms deploying AI must keep records, platforms running algorithms must file. This time it lands on the regulators themselves first — materials screened by AI in a first pass get re-checked by humans, and every step can be traced back.

This is almost the same rule I keep in my own tools. When I wrote a scoring tool for bar-exam subjective questions I set one iron rule: scoring results must come with an evidence chain — which exam point, which sentence fell short — the rationale laid out for human review, never a bare "pass" as output. Writing logs for my auto-iteration tool, installing anti-cheat anchors in my evaluations: same instinct. Every step where AI steps in must leave traces a human can come back and inspect. When the regulators put the same lock on their own AI, the document's credibility goes up.

## "Assistive positioning" is the most valuable phrase

The second qualifier is harder: the opinions make explicit AI's "assistive positioning" in the drug regulation field, and call for completing the whole-process management mechanism.

Translated into the language of liability: AI is the assistant, and a human is the one who signs. The last signature on a review opinion still belongs to the reviewer; the risk assessments AI produces are advisory only. Whoever finally signs off carries the responsibility.

This line of thinking is fully in sync with several judicial threads this year. The two-instance judgments in March's Baidu AI hallucination case ran on the logic "content processed and synthesized by the platform, the platform is liable"; the SPC's 24 opinions in September took "people first" as the first principle. Stack the three documents together and one line is clear: AI may enter the process, but responsibility must land on a person — and the landing point is "who signs".

## For the regulated, the rules of the game are changing

Once the regulatory side runs on AI, the regulated side's situation changes too.

When the first pass your filing faces is an AI, the materials must withstand being read by a machine: structured, uniform in caliber, verifiable. With big-data risk grading on the enforcement side, inspection shifts from "periodic net-casting" to "precision strikes", and compliance stops being the business of surviving inspections and becomes a daily state of data staying continuously online.

The questions I handle over and over in my legal tools — can the materials be verified by a machine, can conclusions be traced back to their sources — become the administrative counterpart's daily homework here. In a sense, compliance in every industry is migrating toward "machine-readable"; the legal industry's documents, evidence and statute citations will be no exception.

## Closing

AI governance is turning into a symmetric problem: regulators watch the regulated with AI, the regulated answer the regulators with AI, and both sides must pass the same three gates — audit trail, verifiability, assistive positioning. Whoever turns these three things into product capability first will stand firm on both sides. The NMPA's document deserves to be read as a forward-looking manual that crosses industries.
