Among the traps in a jurisdictional objection, one of the easiest to step on is reading the phrase "real-estate dispute" too broadly.

The house sits in place A, and buyer and seller are in places B and C. Someone will declare on the spot: it involves real estate, so you can only sue where the property sits — at place A. It sounds airtight, and it does not hold.

Exclusive jurisdiction is the hardest category in statutory jurisdiction: it excludes the parties' agreements, and it excludes the application of general territorial jurisdiction. Precisely because it is hard, its interpretation must stay narrow. The Supreme People's Court has drawn the boundary clearly with five rulings: the following disputes are all not real-estate disputes, and the exclusive jurisdiction of the property's location does not apply.

## First read the statute: the definition is closed

Item 1 of Article 34 of the Civil Procedure Law of the People's Republic of China (《中华人民共和国民事诉讼法》) provides that litigation arising from a real-estate dispute is under the jurisdiction of the people's court at the place where the real estate is located.

But what counts as a "real-estate dispute", the Civil Procedure Law itself does not say. Article 28 of the Supreme People's Court's Interpretation on the Application of the Civil Procedure Law (《最高人民法院关于适用〈中华人民共和国民事诉讼法〉的解释》) supplies the definition, in three paragraphs.

Paragraph 1 limits the scope: a real-estate dispute refers only to property-rights disputes arising from confirmation or division of rights in real estate, neighboring relations, and the like.

Paragraph 2 makes four deemed categories: rural land contract management contract disputes, housing lease contract disputes, construction contract disputes, and policy-based housing sale contract disputes — for these, jurisdiction is determined as for real-estate disputes.

Paragraph 3 fixes how to locate "the place": where the real estate is registered, the location on the register governs; where unregistered, the actual location governs.

Set the two side by side and the threshold shows. Either the dispute falls within the property-rights disputes of paragraph 1, or it squeezes into the four named contract categories of paragraph 2. Touch neither, and exclusive jurisdiction never engages.

### An erratum on an article number reused for years

Plenty of older articles online — including posts from law-firm accounts — cite "item 1 of Article 33 of the Civil Procedure Law". That number belongs to the 2012 and 2017 amended versions. After the fourth amendment in December 2021 (effective January 1, 2022), the Civil Procedure Law's articles shifted forward as a whole: exclusive jurisdiction moved from Article 33 to Article 34, and choice-of-court agreements correspondingly from Article 34 to Article 35. The Interpretation's own citation of Article 28 was synchronized to "item 1 of Article 34".

Old article numbers inside adjudication documents are historical record and should not be altered; but articles cited today, in articles or in representations to court, should use the current numbers.

## Five rulings, five situations where exclusive jurisdiction does not apply

### One, joint real-estate development contract disputes

Disputes arising from the joint development of a real-estate project are not among the real-estate dispute types enumerated in Article 28 of the Interpretation, so the exclusive jurisdiction over real estate does not apply.

Case source: jurisdiction ruling in Yuan Baogen, Li Haisheng and others v. Shanxi Shijing Real Estate Development Co., Ltd., Shanxi Fuyangtai Real Estate Operation Co., Ltd. and others, Supreme People's Court (2016) Zui Gao Fa Min Xia Zhong No. 73.

### Two, real-estate development management rights disputes

Two layers here. First, a real-estate development management right is not a real right in real estate; disputes around it are neither property-rights disputes in real estate nor among the types determined as real-estate disputes, so exclusive jurisdiction has no room to apply. Second, if the subject matter of the suit is itself the ownership of a project's development management right, the amount in controversy is set at the value of that development management right.

Case source: Supreme People's Court (2018) Zui Gao Fa Min Xia Zhong No. 435.

### Three, housing sale contract disputes (the most frequently asked category)

The real-estate disputes subject to exclusive jurisdiction, as drawn by Article 28 of the Interpretation, are limited to "property-rights disputes arising from confirmation or division of rights in real estate, neighboring relations, and the like". The contract at issue changes the ownership of real estate through a sale — essentially a creditor's-rights dispute arising from a contractual relationship — so the exclusive jurisdiction of item 1 of Article 34 of the Civil Procedure Law does not apply.

Case source: civil ruling of the Supreme People's Court (2018) Zui Gao Fa Min Shen No. 1175.

Where this category gets misread most often is the four deemed categories in paragraph 2. What it lists is "policy-based housing sale contract disputes" — mind the modifier. Ordinary commodity housing sales and second-hand housing sales are not among them; only transactions in policy-based housing such as affordable housing and price-capped commodity housing have room to apply. Conflating the two is why plenty of jurisdictional objections go sideways.

### Four, contract disputes arising from taking real estate against debt

A factoring contract itself carries the character of an assignment of claims. Although it contains content about taking real estate against debt, the dispute in fact arises from the assigned claims being satisfied — a contract dispute, not a property-rights dispute formed from the confirmation of rights in real estate — so it is not bound by the exclusive jurisdiction over real estate.

Case source: Supreme People's Court (2017) Zui Gao Fa Min Xia Zhong No. 234.

### Five, disputes arising from joint development of a shantytown renovation project

Same application of Article 28's definition: the dispute arose from the joint development of a "shantytown renovation project" and is not among the real-estate dispute types that Interpretation enumerates.

Case source: Supreme People's Court (2015) Zui Gao Fa Min Xia Zhong No. 73.

## The five rulings are really making one point

The causes of action span joint development, development management rights, housing sales, factoring, and shantytown renovation, yet the landing point is identical: the test is whether the claim's basis lies in a real right or in a creditor's right.

The justification for exclusive jurisdiction over real estate is that having the court at the property's location facilitates inspection and enforcement. That argument holds only while the dispute points at the thing itself. Once the dispute points at contractual obligations to perform, the house is merely subject matter or collateral, the convenience rationale breaks, and jurisdiction returns to the general territorial rules.

So in practice you can judge in three steps:

Step one, does the claim point to confirmation or division of property rights, or to neighboring relations? If yes, exclusive jurisdiction.

Step two, does the cause of action fall within the four deemed categories of paragraph 2? If yes, exclusive jurisdiction.

Step three, if neither, it is an ordinary contract or property-interest dispute — locate it by connecting points such as the defendant's domicile or the place of contract performance; where the parties have a written agreement, a valid agreement prevails.

Step three carries a reverse reminder: once exclusive jurisdiction fails to hold, the space for choice-of-court agreements reopens. A clause in the contract such as "the court at the plaintiff's domicile has jurisdiction" is valid in cases of this kind.
