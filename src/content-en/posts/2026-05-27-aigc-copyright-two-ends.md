Two copyright cases involving AI this year read especially well side by side.

The first was tried by the Jiang'an District Court in Wuhan: a company used generative AI to assist in creating a 47-episode micro short drama; one day after release, someone renamed it, moved it onto their own video channel, and ran ads over it for profit. The court held the drama was an audiovisual work protected by copyright law, and the mover infringed.

Flip back to April and the Beijing High People's Court's annual list of typical IP cases includes a criminal one: the defendants used Stable Diffusion to generate infringing images, made them into jigsaw puzzles, and sold them through e-commerce shops — more than 3,000 units sold, unlawful business volume of over 270,000 yuan. The court found the crime of copyright infringement and sentenced the two principal offenders to one year and six months in prison each.

One case issues a certificate to AI creation; the other convicts AI piracy. Read together, the two judgments give the full map of copyright rules for AI-generated content.

## The rights end: the court counts human participation

The core dispute in the short-drama case is the classic one: does AI-assisted output count as a work?

Copyright law wants "original intellectual achievements of a human being". The Jiang'an court walked the whole creation pipeline: early-stage script planning and copywriting; mid-stage storyboard prompt design, selection of character designs and scene assets, screening of the AI-generated segments; late-stage editing of footage, detail correction, matching of soundtrack and subtitles. Who actually held the pen for the final frames turned out to be the least important question. Conclusion: the creators participated in the full pipeline, invested continuous and substantive intellectual labor, and had complete foresight and substantive control over the expression of the finished piece. The AI tool was a technical means for realizing creative intent; the work belongs to the humans.

This review approach in fact continues the logic of the Beijing Internet Court's 2023 first AI image copyright case: what matters is how much original human input went in. At the scale of a micro drama, the court simply drew up a "participation checklist" and counted the human contributions item by item.

The practical takeaway for creators is direct: your prompt iteration history, your asset screening process, your editing project files — all of them are rights-establishment evidence for the future.

## The criminal end: AI cannot wash away a missing license chain

The jigsaw case runs in exactly the opposite direction. The defendants presumably thought "the images were AI-generated by me" could launder the source. The court's finding was precisely the reverse: using AI as a tool to copy and distribute another's artworks without license and to profit from it, in serious circumstances, is the crime of copyright infringement. The Beijing High People's Court put it bluntly in the typical-significance section: AI technology is no "shield" for infringement.

The details of this case are worth every creator remembering: the victims were original illustrators, and the infringement chain was full-pipeline division of labor — steal images, AI-process, sell on e-commerce; some generated, some ran the shop, some handled customer service. The role of AI processing here was re-skinning stolen images so that "unlicensed copying" would look like new creation. Criminal law does not look at the skin; it looks at the license chain.

## Join the two ends and one complete rule line stands up

Lay the two judgments on top of each other and the rules take shape, with the two directions interlocking:

Forward: human substantive input decides whether AI-generated content gets protection — the deeper the input and the firmer the control, the steadier the right.

Reverse: the license chain decides whether you may feed other people's work to the AI — without a license, the AI is just an instrument of infringement, and it can rise to criminal liability.

Put together in one sentence: AI-generated content has always lived within the reach of copyright law. It neither auto-generates rights for the lazy nor offers technical shelter to the movers.

## A note for tool builders

Building legal tools, I keep running into the same boundary question: for output with AI in the loop, where do responsibility and rights land. These two judgments sketch the judicial answer's early form, and by the way set a requirement for tool design.

Creation tools should make the "human intervention points" visible and traceable — that is accumulating rights-establishment evidence on the user's behalf. Asset tools should expose license status at the intake — that is keeping the user clear of criminal risk. An unremarkable "source" field in a toolbar may one day be a key line of fact in a judgment.

## Closing

In five months, each end placed a stone. Creators received the reassurance: deeply participated AI works are protected. Movers received the warning: switching generators does not change the nature of infringement. Copyright law's hundred-year-old logic — originality and licensing — is taking over this new species in smooth order.
